Level 7 · Lesson 7.3 · about 20 minutes

Transformers: changing flux, changing voltage

A transformer has no moving parts, yet it turns 230 V mains into the 12 V a doorbell needs. The secret is a magnetic flux that never stops changing.

Presets:

What you are seeing

  1. The AC supply pushes a current that reverses 50 times a second (we slowed it 100× so you can follow it). The cyan dots show the primary current changing direction.
  2. That current magnetises the iron core. The magenta arrows show the flux Φ: it grows, shrinks and reverses in step with the current.
  3. The same changing flux passes through every turn of the secondary coil, so each turn gets an induced emf. More turns, more voltage: that is why the lamp's brightness follows Ns.
  4. Switch to DC: the flux rises once and then stays steady. Steady flux induces nothing, so the lamp flashes once and goes dark.

Real world

Power stations send energy at very high voltage so the current, and the I²R heating loss in the cables, stays small. Transformers near your street step it back down.

Derive it yourself

Faraday's law for one coil. A coil of N turns in a changing flux gets an induced emf
ε = −N dΦ/dt
Units: Φ in weber (Wb = V s), t in seconds, so ε comes out in volts. The minus sign is Lenz's law: the emf opposes the change.
One flux, two coils. In an ideal core, every bit of flux made by the primary also passes through the secondary. So both coils see the same dΦ/dt.
Write it for each coil (sizes only, ignoring coil resistance):
Vp = Np dΦ/dt   and   Vs = Ns dΦ/dt
Divide one by the other. The unknown dΦ/dt cancels:
Vs / Vp = Ns / Np
More secondary turns than primary turns steps the voltage up; fewer steps it down.
Energy is conserved. An ideal transformer wastes nothing, so power in equals power out:
VpIp = VsIs  ⇒  Is / Ip = Np / Ns
Stepping voltage up steps current down by the same factor. No free energy.
Why DC fails. Steady DC gives steady flux, so dΦ/dt = 0 and ε = 0. Try the DC button above to watch it happen.

Worked example

A doorbell transformer steps 230 V mains down to 12 V. Its primary has 1150 turns. How many turns does the secondary need?

Ns = Np × Vs / Vp = 1150 × 12 V / 230 V = 60 turns
The volts cancel, leaving a pure number of turns. Check: 60/1150 = 12/230 ≈ 0.052. ✔

Common mistake

“A step-up transformer gives you more energy.”
It gives more voltage but less current. Power out can never be more than power in. Real transformers lose 2–5 % as heat in the core and windings.

2 · Interactive learning and rewards

Activity types

  • Quick checks inside lessons: 1–2 questions right after a new idea.
  • Topic problems: numeric answers with a unit picker, so units are graded too.
  • Practical tasks: steer a simulation to hit a target (for example, land a projectile 30 m away).
  • Projects: multi-goal builds such as the study-desk circuit, checked goal by goal.
  • Diagram answers: free-body diagrams, ray diagrams and circuits, checked by rules.

Points and badges

  • XP only for a first correct answer, so retrying is free and farming is pointless.
  • No penalty for wrong answers. Hints are free; the goal is learning, not gaming.
  • Six ranks named after physics: Spark, Current, Voltage, Field, Flux and Quantum.
  • One badge per level, plus badges for habits: exploring every simulation, scoring 80 % on a mock test.
  • Streaks count days with at least one finished activity, and they pause on exam days instead of breaking.

Notation rules

  • Quantity symbols are italic (v, F, m); unit symbols are upright (m, kg, s). Descriptive subscripts are upright too: Vs for “secondary”.
  • A space separates number and unit: 9.8 m s⁻², 230 V, 25 °C. Unit symbols never take a plural “s” or a full stop.
  • Scientific notation is a × 10n with 1 ≤ |a| < 10; the number of digits shown is the number of significant figures (6.50 × 10⁻⁷ m has three).
  • Vectors are bold or carry an arrow (F or F⃗); a plain F means the magnitude.
  • The course uses g = 9.8 m s⁻² throughout.
  • Every formula is shown with what each symbol means and its unit, and every derivation lists its assumptions (for example “ideal core, no resistance”).

5 · Diagram-based practice

Drawing tools

  • Arrow tool with a label picker (W, N, f, T, F) for force and velocity diagrams.
  • Ray tool with a draggable handle and a protractor read-out for optics.
  • Circuit board with wires, cells, lamps, resistors, switches, ammeters and voltmeters that snap to a grid.
  • Shapes and labels (boxes, circles, text tags) for free sketches in later levels.
  • Every tool works by mouse, touch and keyboard, and every drawn object is also listed as text.

How answers are checked

  • Force diagrams: each arrow's direction within ±12° of the correct one; extra or doubled forces are named (for example, a “sliding force” that is really a component of the weight).
  • Ray diagrams: the angle is compared with Snell's law, n1 sin θ1 = n2 sin θ2, within ±3°.
  • Circuits: the board is solved with nodal analysis. Goals test lamp power, whether a switch really controls each lamp (by simulating it open and closed), meter placement and short circuits.
  • Feedback names the specific object that is wrong and the physics reason, never just “incorrect”.

6 · Mini mock tests

  • Shape: 8–10 questions, 10 minutes, one per topic, with a flag-for-review option and no negative marking.
  • Distractor mapping: every wrong option is written to match one known misconception (inverting a ratio, forgetting a square root, confusing a third-law pair). Choosing it triggers that misconception's explanation.
  • Recommendation engine: each question carries a topic tag and a review link. The results page ranks topics by mistakes and suggests the two most useful things to review next.
  • Retakes shuffle the option order so the test checks understanding, not memory of positions.

7 · Learner-friendly design system

Colours

Type and layout

  • Unbounded for headings, Atkinson Hyperlegible for reading (designed for low-vision readers), STIX Two for maths, JetBrains Mono for read-outs.
  • Body text 16 px or larger, lines under 75 characters, one main action per panel.
  • Every symbol is introduced in words the first time it appears on a page.
  • Layouts collapse to one column on phones; diagrams scroll inside their own frame.

Accessibility and motion

  • Text contrast of at least 4.5 : 1; colour is never the only signal (ticks, labels and patterns too).
  • All controls reachable by keyboard with a visible focus ring; touch targets at least 44 px.
  • Results and feedback announced to screen readers through live regions.
  • Only one simulation animates at a time, each has a pause button, and “reduce motion” settings are honoured.
  • Animations explain something; nothing moves just to decorate a lesson page.

8 · Build plan

Technology

  • Front end: React + TypeScript; simulations on Canvas 2D, diagrams in SVG (both are used in this prototype).
  • Maths typesetting: KaTeX in production for full equation layout.
  • Circuit solver: modified nodal analysis, the same method used here, run in a Web Worker for large boards.
  • Accounts and progress: a hosted database (for example Supabase) storing activity results and misconception tags.
  • Content: lessons written in MDX with embedded simulation components, reviewed by a physics teacher before release.

Phases
